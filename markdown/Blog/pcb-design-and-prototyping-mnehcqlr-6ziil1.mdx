**Prototyping** is not a single milestone—it is a **sequence of bets**. Each **spin** (board order) should answer **specific questions**. When teams treat every revision as “fix everything,” schedules slip and changes correlate poorly with root causes. This article outlines common prototype goals, what each can validate, and what still requires other work (software, mechanical, compliance).

## Rev A — “Does the scary thing work?”

The first boards often target **risk reduction**:

- New **PMIC** or **RF** front end,
- **DDR** or **MIPI** layout you have not built before,
- **Connector** or **module** that is new to your supply chain.

**Scope** the BOM for learning: you might use **known-good** parts for non-critical sections while you stress-test the risky ones. Document **assumptions** (“RF match not final,” “heatsink TBD”) so the team does not confuse prototype results with production readiness.

## Electrical validation — what you can and cannot prove

### What quick-turn boards validate well

- **Power** rails under load (with appropriate loads and measurement),
- **Digital** bring-up: JTAG/SWD, flash, **clock** trees,
- **Interface** bring-up: link training, eye checks with lab equipment,
- **Basic** SI/PI sanity when paired with simulation or comparison to reference designs.

### What usually needs more than one spin

- **EMC** pre-compliance: layout changes often follow first radiated scan,
- **Thermal** extremes: airflow, enclosure, and **fan** curves interact,
- **Manufacturing** yield: **DFM** tweaks after assembly feedback.

## Mechanical and fit prototypes

Sometimes you order a **bare board** or **partial assembly** to check:

- **Enclosure** standoffs, **keep-out**, and **connector** alignment,
- **Button** travel and **display** window cutouts,
- **Antenna** clearance and **metal** proximity.

3D-printed housings iterate in parallel; **tolerance stack** matters—CAD perfect ≠ assembled perfect.

## Cost-down and value engineering

Later spins often focus on:

- **Second-source** passives and **alternates** for long-lead ICs,
- **Layer count** reduction once routing is proven,
- **Panelization** for **volume** assembly.

Do not optimize cost on **rev A** if it blocks learning—**premature** cost-down hides schedule risk behind “we saved five cents.”

## Documentation that pays off across spins

Maintain a **living** document per project:

- **BOM** revision history and **why** parts changed,
- **Known issues** list with **workarounds** (firmware or assembly),
- **Test procedures** so the next engineer can reproduce measurements.

When **field failures** appear, this history separates “we introduced a bug in rev C” from “environmental stress we never tested.”

## Timeline planning — realistic expectations

**Quick-turn** fabs compress **calendar** time, but **your** schedule still includes:

- **Assembly** queue,
- **Bring-up** and **debug**,
- **Firmware** releases gated on hardware findings,
- **Retest** after each meaningful change.

Assume **multiple** spins for anything with **RF**, **high-speed digital**, or **strict EMC**—not because the team is slow, because **physics** and **factories** have variance.

## Summary

Treat each prototype as an **experiment** with a **hypothesis** and **pass/fail** criteria. You will ship faster when revisions are **purposeful**, documented, and sized to answer real risks—not every problem at once.
